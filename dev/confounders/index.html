3. Separating the confounders from the effects

This page works on a Gaussian scale: a matrix $Y$ ($n$ samples $\times$ $p$ genes) of log abundances, or of the latent means of the PLN fit (§5). For a gene $j$:

\[Y_{\cdot j} = X \beta_j + Z \Gamma_j + E_{\cdot j}, \qquad Z = X\alpha + W, \qquad E_{\cdot j} \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_j^2 I_n),\]

with $Z$ the $n \times r$ matrix of hidden factors (the offset factor included, with $\Gamma_{1j} = 1$).

3.1 The rotation

The design is split as $X = [X_1\; X_2]$: nuisance covariates $X_1$ ($k_1$ columns, with the intercept) and the covariate of interest $X_2$ ($k_2$ columns, usually one). Write the QR decomposition $X = QR$, with $Q$ an $n \times n$ orthogonal matrix and $R$ upper triangular, and rotate the data: $\tilde Y = Q^\top Y$. Because $Q$ is orthogonal, the noise $Q^\top E_{\cdot j}$ is still $\mathcal N(0, \sigma_j^2 I_n)$. The rows of $\tilde Y$ split into three blocks (Wang et al. 2017):

rowsnumbercontent
nuisance$k_1$$R_{11}$(nuisance coefficients) $+ \dots$: carries no information on $\beta_2$, dropped
interest$k_2$$R_{22}\,(\beta_{2j} + \alpha_2^\top \Gamma_j) + u^\top\Gamma_j +$ noise
residual$n - k$$\tilde W \Gamma_j +$ noise, with no trace of $X$

Result — the residual rows see the confounders but not the effects. A factor analysis on them estimates the loadings $\Gamma_j$ and the variances $\sigma_j^2$ without any risk of absorbing the effects of interest. This is how Wang et al. avoid the main pitfall of factor-based corrections (factors that "explain away" the treatment).

Derivation

$Q^\top X = R$, which is zero below row $k$. The rows $k+1, \dots, n$ of $Q^\top Y_{\cdot j}$ are therefore $Q_{\text{res}}^\top W \Gamma_j + Q_{\text{res}}^\top E_{\cdot j}$: the design has disappeared. Row $k_1 + 1$ (for $k_2 = 1$) is $R_{22}(\beta_{2j} + \alpha_2^\top\Gamma_j) + q^\top W \Gamma_j + q^\top E_{\cdot j}$, with $q$ the corresponding column of $Q$; the nuisance coefficients do not appear because $R$ is upper triangular. The term $u^\top \Gamma_j = q^\top W \Gamma_j$ is the realisation of the factors in that row. It is the same vector $u$ for every gene, so dividing by $R_{22}$, it is absorbed into the confounder coefficient: $\alpha \leftarrow \alpha_2 + u / R_{22}$. The $\alpha$ estimated below is this data-specific value, which is what removes the confounding in this data set.

Dividing the row of interest by $R_{22}$ gives, for each gene, a confounded estimate and its standard error:

\[\hat{\tilde\beta}_j = \tilde Y_{\text{int}, j} / R_{22} \;\sim\; \mathcal N\!\big(\beta_j + \alpha^\top\Gamma_j,\; s_j^2\big), \qquad s_j = \sigma_j / \lvert R_{22}\rvert .\]

3.2 Factor analysis on the residual rows

The residual block $Y_{\text{res}}$ ($(n - k) \times p$) is fitted by a heteroscedastic factor model, $Y_{\text{res}} \approx L\,\Gamma$, with gene-specific residual variances $\sigma_j^2$. The fit alternates two steps, in the spirit of the early-stopping alternation (ESA) of Owen & Wang (2016):

  1. with the variances fixed, the rank-$q$ approximation that minimises $\sum_j \lVert Y_{\text{res},j} - L \Gamma_j\rVert^2 / \sigma_j^2$ is a singular value decomposition of $Y_{\text{res}}\,\text{diag}(\sigma)^{-1}$; the offset factor (loadings one) is fitted first, as a weighted mean of each row;
  2. with the fit fixed, $\sigma_j^2$ is the residual sum of squares divided by its $n - k - q - 1$ degrees of freedom.

Number of factors. choose_q chooses $q$ by count splitting (Neufeld et al. 2024): every count is split in two parts, the PLN model is fitted on the first for each $q$ and scored by the predictive log-likelihood of the second, where each count is $\text{Poisson}(e^{o_i + Z_{ij}})$ with a fresh $Z_{ij} \sim \mathcal N(\mu_{ij}, \sigma_j^2)$ (Gauss–Hermite quadrature).

Binomial thinning, $Y^{(1)} \sim \text{Binomial}(Y, \varepsilon)$, gives two parts that are independent given the latent values, but they share the latent overdispersion: when it dominates the Poisson noise (counts of a few tens and more), a low-rank mean that has learned it is rewarded on the second part, and too many factors are chosen. choose_q therefore splits by beta-binomial thinning (data thinning, Neufeld, Dharamshi, Gao & Witten 2024), $Y^{(1)} \mid Y \sim \text{BetaBinomial}(Y, \varepsilon r_j, (1 - \varepsilon) r_j)$. For a count that is Poisson given a rate of mean $m$ and variance $v$,

\[\operatorname{Cov}(Y^{(1)}, Y^{(2)}) = \varepsilon (1 - \varepsilon)\, \frac{v\, r - m^2}{r + 1},\]

which vanishes at $r = m^2 / v = 1 / \mathrm{CV}^2$. For the lognormal rate of gene $j$ this is $r_j = 1 / (e^{\sigma_j^2} - 1)$, whatever the mean; binomial thinning is the limit $r \to \infty$. The two parts are then uncorrelated (independent for a gamma rate), and an overestimated $\sigma_j^2$ ($r_j$ too small) makes them negatively correlated, which can only favour fewer factors. $\sigma_j^2$ comes from a fit at the current choice of $q$, starting from $q = 0$ (whose $\sigma_j^2$ contains the factors' variance: conservative), and the choice is repeated until it is stable. With one or two residual degrees of freedom, a fit with factors can diverge on a split (a gene whose training part is zero in some samples lets a loading push those cells towards $-\infty$); such a $q$ is rejected, the data on half the counts not supporting it, so the choice falls back towards $q = 0$, which has no loadings and cannot diverge. Priors on the loadings, the planned fix, would remove the divergence itself.

On PLN simulations with two factors (20 samples), binomial thinning chooses $q = 4$ at counts around 20 and 150; the beta-binomial split chooses $q = 2$ from counts of about 3 upwards, in 2–3 rounds. At counts below 1 it stays at $q = 0$: there the latent noise cannot be identified, and with $q = 0$ its estimate absorbs the factors' variance, which errs on the conservative side. With very few samples it also prevents factors fitted to noise: with 3 samples per group, $q = 2$ leaves one residual degree of freedom and an FDP of 36–41 % on PLN-exact data, against 7–13 % with the $q$ chosen. Its behaviour on thinned GTEx has not been re-evaluated yet.

3.3 Identifying the confounders

The confounded estimates follow a regression with unknown coefficients $\alpha$ and known regressors $\Gamma_j$: $\hat{\tilde\beta}_j = \beta_j + \Gamma_j^\top\alpha + \text{noise}$. Two assumptions identify $\alpha$:

  • negative controls (RUV-4, Gagnon-Bartsch et al. 2013): for $j \in C$, $\beta_j = 0$, so a regression of $\hat{\tilde\beta}_C$ on $\Gamma_C$ estimates $\alpha$. It needs at least $q + 1$ controls (one per factor, the offset factor included).
  • sparsity (CATE-RR of Wang et al., MOUTHWASH of Gerard & Stephens 2020): most $\beta_j$ are zero, so $\alpha$ is the value that makes the residuals $\hat{\tilde\beta}_j - \Gamma_j^\top\alpha$ as concentrated at zero as possible. MOUTHWASH makes this precise with a likelihood, §4.2.

Result — the compositional shift is estimated like any other confounder: it is the coefficient $\alpha_1$ of the offset factor, whose regressor is $\Gamma_{1j} = 1$. With controls, it is the average confounded effect of the controls, once the other factors are removed. Without controls, it is the mode of the effects, as in LinDA (Zhou et al. 2022) or ANCOM-BC, but estimated jointly with the other confounders.

3.4 Why a fixed offset factor rather than one more free factor

A free factor could, in principle, learn loadings close to one. But its loadings are estimated from the residual rows, where the compositional shift may be weak or absent: the shift correlated with the design lives in the row of interest. With the loadings fixed, nothing has to be learned, and the shift is captured exactly. On simulations with a shift correlated with the group (§7.2), a free extra factor leaves an RMSE of 0.15–0.26 and a false discovery proportion of 6–15 %; the fixed offset factor gives 0.10–0.12 and 3–7 %.
