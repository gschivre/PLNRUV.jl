PLNRUV.jl

Differential expression from RNA-seq counts, adjusted for hidden confounders and for the compositional nature of sequencing.

An RNA-seq experiment compares the expression of thousands of genes between conditions. Two things make the comparison harder than it looks:

  • Hidden confounders. Batch, the time a sample spent on the bench, the cell-type mix of a tissue: they shift the expression of many genes at once. When they are correlated with the condition, they create false differences, or hide true ones.
  • Composition. A sequencer reads a fixed budget of molecules from each library. The reads tell us how the RNA of a sample is shared among genes, not how much RNA there is. If a few genes are induced massively, every other gene loses reads, and looks repressed. And when the whole transcriptome rises or falls, as in stem cells, cancers, quiescence or a seedling exposed to light, standard normalisation hides the change.

This package handles both with a single device. It models the counts with a Poisson-lognormal (PLN) distribution. It treats the hidden confounders as latent factors, following Wang et al.'s CATE and Gerard & Stephens' RUVB and MOUTHWASH. And it shows that the compositional offset is just one more latent factor, whose loadings are known: it shifts every gene of a sample by the same amount. Negative control genes, or the assumption that most genes do not change, identify all of these factors at once.

The story in five steps

  1. The problem. Counts, compositions, and confounders. Why the change of a gene is not identified from the reads alone, and what restores it.
  2. The model. Independent Poisson counts on top of a latent Gaussian layer: a regression on the design, latent factors, and gene-specific overdispersion. The compositional offset becomes a factor with loadings fixed to one.
  3. Separating the confounders from the effects. The rotation of Wang et al. splits the samples into a part that carries the effects and a part that only sees the confounders. Controls or sparsity then pin down the confounders.
  4. Empirical Bayes: ash, MOUTHWASH and variance shrinkage. The prior on the effects, the confounders and the variances are all estimated from the data. No prior to choose.
  5. Fitting the Poisson-lognormal layer. A variational EM whose E-step has a closed form (Lambert W), and whose M-step is the Gaussian machinery of step 3.

Then: simulating data by binomial thinning, validation, assumptions and limits, notation and references, and the API.

How to read this manual

The main text is written for a biologist who is comfortable with probabilities. It explains every idea in words, then states it as an equation. Each modelling choice is labelled:

  • assumption: a choice about the biology or the measurement, which could be wrong;
  • result: a mathematical consequence of the assumptions, shown in the text.

Quick start

using PLNRUV, Random, Distributions

# real counts (genes x samples) from GTEx, thinned to create a two-group experiment with known effects
gtex = read_gct("gene_reads_muscle_skeletal.gct")
M = gtex.counts[filter_genes(gtex.counts; min_mean = 10), :]
sim = thin_2group(Xoshiro(1), M; ngenes = 5_000, nsamp = 20, nctrl = 500, prop_null = 0.8,
    signal = Normal(0, 0.8), target_cor = [0.6, 0.2])   # the group is correlated with two hidden factors

# empirical Bayes branch: PLN fit with 2 latent factors + the compositional offset, then MOUTHWASH
fit = pln_mouthwash(sim.counts, sim.X, 2; controls = sim.controls)
res = fit.mouthwash
res.mean     # posterior mean of each log fold change (natural log; divide by log(2) for log2)
res.lfsr     # local false sign rate: probability that the sign of the effect is wrong
res.svalue   # s-value: declare the genes with svalue < 0.05 to control the false sign rate at 5 %

# without control genes, the sparsity of the effects identifies the confounders
fit_free = pln_mouthwash(sim.counts, sim.X, 2)
