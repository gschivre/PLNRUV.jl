5. Fitting the PLN layer

The latent log-abundances $Z_{ij}$ of §2 are not observed. The likelihood of a count is an integral with no closed form:

\[p(y_{ij} \mid \mu_{ij}, \sigma_j^2) = \int \text{Poisson}\big(y_{ij};\, e^{o_i + z}\big)\, \mathcal N(z;\, \mu_{ij}, \sigma_j^2)\, dz .\]

The package maximises instead a lower bound of the log-likelihood, the ELBO, by variational inference (Chiquet et al. 2018). The fit, plnpca, also treats the factors, the loadings and the variances as random, with priors estimated from all genes. This is variational empirical Bayes. The coefficients have no prior, but their uncertainty is kept.

5.1 The model as a hierarchy

plnpca completes the model of §2 with priors:

  • Factors. The scores of the $q$ hidden factors are random, $\mathcal N(0, 1)$, and live in the residual space of the design ($X^\top W = 0$). Their loadings are learned only from the variation that the design cannot explain, as in the rotation of §3. A factor therefore cannot absorb the effects of interest.
  • Loadings. $\Gamma_{lj} \sim \mathcal N(0, \tau_l^2)$, with one variance per factor. A factor that the data do not support gets $\tau_l^2 \to 0$ and is switched off (automatic relevance determination).
  • Coefficients. $B_j$ has a flat prior: the data alone determine it, and the fit does not depend on how the design is coded. Its uncertainty is kept, which matters for the variances (§5.4).
  • Variances. $\sigma_j^2 \sim \text{InvGamma}(a_0,\, a_0 \sigma_0^2(x_j))$, the limma prior with $d_0 = 2a_0$ (§4.5), whose scale $\sigma_0^2$ is a smooth function of the mean log-abundance $x_j$ of gene $j$ (§5.4).

The offset factor $w_{i0}$ stays a free parameter, also in the residual space of the design. The hyperparameters $\tau_l^2$, $a_0$ and $\sigma_0^2(\cdot)$ are estimated from all genes at every iteration.

5.2 The variational approximation

  • Approximation — the posterior is replaced by a product of simple distributions: a normal $\mathcal N(M_{ij}, S^2_{ij})$ for each latent value, normals for the scores, and for each gene a joint normal–inverse-gamma distribution of its coefficients, loadings and variance.

Only the latent values $Z_{ij}$ see the Poisson counts. Given them, the rest of the model is a linear Gaussian factor model. So every update has a closed form, and each one increases the ELBO, except in the first iterations on low counts (§5.4). The fit stops when the ELBO changes by less than a tolerance per count; the field converged reports whether this happened before maxiter.

5.3 The latent values: a closed form

For one count $y_{ij}$, the ELBO is concave in $M_{ij}$. Result — its maximum has a closed form:

\[M_{ij} = \mu_{ij} + \sigma_j^2 y_{ij} - \mathcal W_0\Big(\sigma_j^2\, e^{\,o_i + \mu_{ij} + \sigma_j^2 y_{ij} + S^2_{ij}/2}\Big),\]

where $\mathcal W_0$ is the Lambert W function (the solution of $w e^w = x$), $\mu_{ij}$ the current mean and $\sigma_j^2$ the current value of $1 / \mathbb E[1/\sigma_j^2]$. The variance is the solution of $S^2_{ij} = 1 / (e^{\,o_i + M_{ij} + S^2_{ij}/2} + 1/\sigma_j^2)$, found by Newton's method on its logarithm. The two are updated in turn, each exactly, so each step increases the ELBO. Repeating the right-hand side instead (a fixed-point iteration) can oscillate with a growing amplitude, for a zero count of a gene with a large variance.

Derivation

The derivative in $M$ is zero when $y - e^{a + M} - (M - \mu)/\sigma^2 = 0$, with $a = o + S^2/2$. Let $u = \mu + \sigma^2 y - M$. The equation becomes $u\,e^{u} = \sigma^2 e^{a + \mu + \sigma^2 y}$, so $u = \mathcal W_0(\sigma^2 e^{a + \mu + \sigma^2 y})$. For large counts the argument overflows. The package therefore computes $\mathcal W_0(e^t)$ directly, by Newton's method on $w + \log w = t$. The derivative in $S^2$ is zero when $1/S^2 = e^{a + M} + 1/\sigma^2$.

5.4 The variances

Degrees of freedom. Gene $j$ has $n$ counts, one per sample, but its own coefficients and loadings use some of them. Their number is the trace of the gene's hat matrix, $t_j \approx k + q$. The posterior of the variance counts only the rest:

\[q(\sigma_j^2) = \text{InvGamma}\Big(a_0 + \tfrac{n - t_j}{2},\;\; a_0 \sigma_0^2(x_j) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\text{RSS}_j\Big),\]

where $\text{RSS}_j$ is the expected residual sum of squares of the latent values. Its mean is the restricted (REML) estimate, as in limma (§4.5).

Why $n - t_j$ and not $n$. A plain product of independent distributions (mean field) for the coefficients and the variance gives the shape $a_0 + n/2$. It treats every count as a free observation of $\sigma_j^2$. The mean is the same, but the posterior is too narrow. With few samples this matters: with 4 samples, 2 of the 4 degrees of freedom go to the coefficients. The estimate of $a_0$ then grows far too large. All variances are pulled to one value, the fit converges very slowly, and low-count genes get false discoveries. The joint normal–inverse-gamma distribution of §5.2 avoids this.

The trend in expression. On real data the variances depend on expression. On GTEx muscle they follow a U-shape: high for weakly expressed genes, lowest in the upper middle, and higher again for the most expressed genes (§7.3). A curve that can only decrease, such as the dispersion trend of DESeq2, does not follow it. The scale $\sigma_0^2$ is therefore a local fit: at each point of a grid over $x$, a local-linear likelihood over the 20 % of genes nearest in expression (span = 0.2), with tricube weights. It is held flat beyond the 1 % and 99 % quantiles of $x$. The covariate $x_j$ is the mean of the initial log counts of gene $j$, fixed during the fit.

var_prior = :common gives a single scale, as limma without trend. It pulls the variances of weakly and highly expressed genes towards the middle.

Degrees of freedom passed on. The shrunk variance is $\sigma_j^2 = 1/\mathbb E[1/\sigma_j^2]$, with $2a_0 + n - t_j$ degrees of freedom. The $t$ likelihood of MOUTHWASH uses them (§4.4).

Counts without information. A count of 0 or 1 says little about its latent value: its variational variance $S^2_{ij}$ stays close to $\sigma_j^2$, and the count only returns the current value of $\sigma_j^2$. Such a count is not an observation of the variance. The shape above therefore also leaves out $\sum_i S^2_{ij}/\sigma_j^2$, the share of the counts that the data leave unresolved: $t_j$ becomes $u_j = t_j + \sum_i S^2_{ij}/\sigma_j^2$, at most $n$. Without this, low counts make the variances look far more certain than they are, $a_0$ grows without bound, and the fit drifts.

The share of uninformative counts depends on $\sigma_j^2$ and $S^2_{ij}$, which are far from each other at the start. With low counts the ELBO can therefore decrease slightly in the first iterations; it then increases.

Weak factors. Scores and loadings are updated in turn, and for a weak factor a change of one is almost undone by the other: alternating updates then take hundreds of small steps. After each iteration, a parameter-expanded step (PX-VB, Qi & Jaakkola 2007) finds the best rescaling and rotation of scores and loadings that leaves their product, and so the fit, unchanged. It cannot decrease the ELBO, and brings such fits from hundreds of iterations to a few dozen.

Slow convergence. Very sparse counts, such as single-cell data, make the fit slow. When all genes have exactly the same variance, $a_0$ grows without bound, which is the right answer, and the trend has nothing to fit: the fit may then stop at maxiter with converged = false. Real data are far from this case.

5.5 From the PLN fit to MOUTHWASH

The effect tested is a contrast $c^\top B_j$: by default the last coefficient, or any combination of them (§5.6). Its raw estimate is a weighted sum of the latent values, $b_j = a^\top M_{\cdot j}$ with $a = X (X^\top X)^{-1} c$. For the last coefficient, $a$ is the last column of $Q$ divided by $R_{kk}$.

Its standard error uses the model's variance of each count on the latent scale: the biological variance plus the Poisson noise, $\sigma_j^2 + 1/\bar y_{ij}$:

\[s_j = \sqrt{\sum_i a_i^2\, \big(\sigma_j^2 + 1/\bar y_{ij}\big)} .\]

The spread of the residual rows of $M$ cannot be used instead: the fit pulls them towards the factors, so their spread underestimates the noise.

Uncertainty of the loadings. The fit also returns the posterior covariance of each gene's loadings, Γcov. MOUTHWASH uses it: the correction $\Gamma_j^\top\alpha$ has the error variance $\alpha^\top \text{Cov}(\Gamma_j)\,\alpha$, which is added to the noise of the gene (§4.3). The estimates $b_j$, their standard errors, the loadings and their covariances then go into MOUTHWASH.

5.6 Designs and contrasts

Any design works: several groups, time points, interactions, covariates. Write it as a formula, then ask questions as contrasts:

using PLNRUV, StatsModels
samples = (genotype = ["wt", "wt", "mut", "mut", ...], time = ["0h", "2h", "0h", "2h", ...])
res = pln_mouthwash(counts, @formula(0 ~ genotype * time), samples, q; controls,
                    contrast = ["genotype: wt & time: 2h" => 1])   # does the time effect differ between genotypes?
res.coefnames                                                      # the names you can use in a contrast
  • A contrast is a coefficient name, or names with weights: ["time: 6h" => 1, "time: 2h" => -1] compares two time points. With a design matrix built by hand, give a vector of weights instead.
  • Several contrasts at once: give a list, and res.mouthwash holds one result per contrast. The PLN fit does not depend on the contrast, so it is done once. test_contrast adds another contrast later in seconds.
  • The answer does not depend on how you code the design. Treatment coding or one mean per group give the same result for the same question.
  • Each contrast gets its own confounder correction, including its own compositional shift $\alpha_0$.

Genes that cannot be tested. A gene with no count in a whole group, level or time point has no finite estimate: the data are compatible with any large enough change. Such genes are left out of the fit and reported as not tested, in res.mouthwash.separated, with no estimate. Often the direction is obvious, but its size and reliability are not: look at these genes directly.

5.7 Without the PLN layer: Gaussian log-ratios

log_ratio followed by the Gaussian mouthwash is a lighter alternative. The counts are turned into log-ratios, $\log(y_{ij} + 0.5)$ minus the mean over a set of reference genes (all genes, or the controls), and analysed as Gaussian data: rotation, factor analysis, variance shrinkage by vash or limma around the same trend in expression as the PLN fit (§5.4), and MOUTHWASH. The offset factor plays the same role as in the PLN fit.

It is fast and works well when counts are high. Its limits:

  • Low counts. The pseudocount and the logarithm distort low counts, and the Poisson noise is not modelled: a gene with 3 reads is treated as precisely as one with 3 000.
  • Fixed loadings. The factor analysis gives point estimates of the loadings. Their uncertainty (§5.5) is not passed to MOUTHWASH, so confounded designs with few samples can be slightly anti-conservative.
  • One column at a time. It tests a column of the design (cov_of_interest), not a contrast (§5.6).
