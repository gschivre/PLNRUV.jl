6. Simulation by binomial thinning

A method for real data should be tested on data that look real: real correlations between genes, real overdispersion, real zeros and real hidden factors. Parametric simulations get some of these wrong. Binomial thinning (Gerard 2020) starts from a real count matrix and adds known effects to it.

6.1 Thinning adds an exact effect

Take two groups of real samples, with $x_i = 1$ in group 1 and $x_i = 0$ in group 0, and a log₂ fold change $\beta_j$. Keep each read of gene $j$ in sample $i$ independently with probability

\[\rho_{ij} = 2^{\,x_i\beta_j - \max_l x_l\beta_j} .\]

Result — if the original count is Poisson given any random mean, the thinned count is Poisson with that mean times $\rho_{ij}$. So the expected count of group 1 is multiplied by exactly $2^{\beta_j}$ relative to group 0. Everything else in the data is kept: correlations, factors, overdispersion.

Thinning the changed genes also lowers the library sizes of the samples where they were thinned. This is the compositional effect of §1.1, created naturally.

6.2 Controls and the truth

A set of genes is drawn as negative controls, with $\beta_j = 0$. To test robustness, controls can also carry small effects (ctrl_signal). Among the other genes, a proportion prop_null is null. The others get effects drawn from signal. Because thinning acts on the expected counts, the true effect of each gene relative to the controls is $\beta_j$.

6.3 Groups correlated with hidden factors

To create confounding, the groups are not assigned at random:

  1. The factors $U$ of the real samples are estimated by a PCA of their log counts. Each sample is centred first, which removes sequencing depth; then each gene is centred.
  2. A score $\zeta_i = U_i^\top h + \sqrt{1 - \lVert h\rVert^2}\,\epsilon_i$, with $\epsilon_i \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$, is drawn for each sample. The samples with the largest scores form group 1.

Result — if a fraction $f$ of the samples is in group 1, the correlation between the group and factor $l$ is $h_l\,\phi(z_f)/\sqrt{f(1-f)}$, where $\phi$ is the standard normal density and $z_f$ its $1 - f$ quantile. The weights $h$ are set to reach the target correlations (target_cor).

Derivation

$U_l$ and $\zeta$ are jointly normal with unit variances and covariance $h_l$. Stein's lemma gives $\mathbb E[U_l \mathbf 1\{\zeta > z_f\}] = h_l\,\phi(z_f)$. The group indicator has variance $f(1-f)$.

The samples are centred on purpose. Sequencing depth is technical, and every method corrects for it. The confounding that matters comes from the biological and unwanted variation of §1.4. seqgendiff does not centre the samples, so its first factor mostly follows depth.

Functions

  • thin_2group: the complete two-group simulation.
  • thin_counts: thinning for any design and coefficient matrix.
  • surrogate_factors, assign_groups: the confounded group assignment.
  • filter_genes: keep the genes observed often enough.
