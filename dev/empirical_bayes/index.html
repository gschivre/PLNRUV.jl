4. Empirical Bayes

A Bayesian answer needs a prior on the effects. A prior chosen by hand is hard to defend. With thousands of genes, the distribution of the effects can instead be estimated from the data, and then used as the prior of each gene. This is empirical Bayes. The package estimates three things this way:

  • the distribution of the effects (ash, §4.1–4.2);
  • the confounders, together with that distribution (MOUTHWASH, §4.3);
  • the distribution of the gene variances (§4.5).

4.1 Adaptive shrinkage (ash)

Start from estimates $b_j$ with standard errors $s_j$: $b_j \sim \mathcal N(\beta_j, s_j^2)$.

  • Assumption — the effects come from a distribution $g$ that is unimodal and centred at zero. It is written as a mixture of normals that includes a point mass at zero (Stephens 2017):

\[g(\beta) = \sum_{l=0}^{L} \pi_l\, \mathcal N(\beta;\, 0,\, \tau_l^2),\qquad \tau_0 = 0 .\]

The standard deviations $\tau_l$ form a fixed, dense grid, from $\min_j s_j / 10$ to $2\sqrt{\max_j (b_j^2 - s_j^2)}$. Only the weights $\pi_l$ are estimated. So $g$ can take almost any unimodal symmetric shape: spiky, heavy-tailed, or all its mass at zero.

The weights maximise the marginal likelihood of the estimates, plus a penalty $9 \log \pi_0$ that favours the point mass at zero (a Dirichlet(10, 1, …, 1) prior). The penalty makes the error rates conservative:

\[\hat\pi = \arg\max_{\pi}\; \sum_j \log \sum_l \pi_l\, \mathcal N\big(b_j;\, 0,\, s_j^2 + \tau_l^2\big) \;+\; 9 \log \pi_0 .\]

This problem is concave. It is solved by mix-SQP (Kim et al. 2020), which converges in a few steps where EM can need thousands.

Posterior of each effect

Given $\hat\pi$, the posterior of $\beta_j$ is again a mixture. Component $l$ has weight $\propto \hat\pi_l\,\mathcal N(b_j; 0, s_j^2 + \tau_l^2)$. For $l \ge 1$ it is a normal with mean $b_j\, \tau_l^2 / (\tau_l^2 + s_j^2)$ and variance $\tau_l^2 s_j^2 / (\tau_l^2 + s_j^2)$. Component 0 is the point mass at zero.

Outputs.

  • The posterior mean of each effect. A noisy estimate is pulled towards zero more than a precise one.
  • The local false sign rate (lfsr): the probability that the sign of the posterior mean is wrong, $\text{lfsr}_j = \min\{P(\beta_j \ge 0 \mid \text{data}),\, P(\beta_j \le 0 \mid \text{data})\}$.
  • The s-value: the average lfsr of all genes at least as significant. Reporting the genes with s-value below 0.05 keeps the expected proportion of wrong signs among them at 5 %.
  • The local false discovery rate $P(\beta_j = 0 \mid \text{data})$ and its q-value.

The lfsr is preferred to the local false discovery rate. A discovery with the wrong sign is an error too. And the lfsr does not depend much on the exact mass at zero, which is hard to estimate.

4.2 A prior for global changes

The unimodal prior of §4.1 assumes that most genes do not change. Suppose instead that the controls are valid and that most other genes go up together. The effects are then a point mass at zero (the unchanged genes) plus a bump around a common shift $\bar\beta$ (figure of §1.3). A prior centred at zero can reach the bump only with wide components. These also cover small values, so they take mass from the point mass at zero. The null genes then look less null than they are, and false discoveries increase.

With mode = :auto, the non-null part of the prior is centred at an estimated $\bar\beta$. The point mass stays at zero, where the controls put it:

\[g(\beta) = \pi_0\, \delta_0 + \sum_{l=1}^{L} \pi_l\, \mathcal N(\beta;\, \bar\beta,\, \tau_l^2) .\]

ash in R has a similar option, mode = "estimate", but it moves the point mass to $\bar\beta$ too. That makes the bulk of changing genes the reference, which is what the controls are meant to avoid.

$\bar\beta$ maximises the marginal likelihood. It is used only when it improves the fit of genes it was not fitted on. The genes are split in two halves. Both priors are fitted on one half and scored on the other. The shifted prior is kept only if it gains more than mode_threshold = 0.05 in log-likelihood per gene. Otherwise the prior is the unimodal one of §4.1.

This option needs controls. Without controls, a shift shared by most genes cannot be told apart from the compositional offset $\alpha_0$. Sparsity then sets the most common change to zero, by definition.

4.3 MOUTHWASH: confounders and effects together

After the rotation of §3, each gene has a raw estimate $b_j \sim \mathcal N(\beta_j + \Gamma_j^\top\alpha,\; \lambda s_j^2)$. Gerard & Stephens (2020) estimate $\alpha$ inside the ash likelihood:

\[(\hat\pi, \hat\alpha, \hat\lambda) = \arg\max\; \sum_{j \notin C}\log\sum_l \pi_l\,\mathcal N\big(b_j - \Gamma_j^\top\alpha;\; 0,\; \lambda s_j^2 + \tau_l^2\big) + \sum_{j \in C}\log\mathcal N\big(b_j - \Gamma_j^\top\alpha;\; 0,\; \lambda s_j^2\big) + 9\log\pi_0 .\]

The second sum is over the controls, whose prior is the point mass at zero. Without controls, only the point mass of $g$ identifies $\alpha$ (sparsity). With controls, $\alpha$ is fitted on the controls only, by weighted least squares. This stays valid when most other genes change.

The variance inflation $\lambda$ absorbs what the standard errors miss. It is a single factor for all genes.

The maximisation alternates three steps:

  1. $\pi$ by mix-SQP, with $\alpha$ and $\lambda$ fixed;
  2. $\alpha$ by weighted least squares (without controls);
  3. $\lambda$ by a one-dimensional search.

It stops when the objective changes by less than a relative tolerance, in either direction. The field converged of the result reports whether this happened before the iteration limit.

The effects are then shrunk by ash, applied to the corrected estimates $b_j - \Gamma_j^\top\hat\alpha$.

Uncertainty of $\hat\alpha$. The corrected estimates share the error of $\hat\alpha$. Its variance is added to each gene: $s_j^2 + \Gamma_j^\top V_\alpha \Gamma_j$, where $V_\alpha$ is the covariance of $\hat\alpha$ (as in Wang et al. 2017). The grid of $\tau_l$ is built from these standard errors.

Uncertainty of the loadings. The loadings are estimated too. When the PLN fit provides their posterior covariance $\text{Cov}(\Gamma_j)$ (§5.5), MOUTHWASH uses it twice:

  • the correction $\Gamma_j^\top\hat\alpha$ has the error variance $\hat\alpha^\top \text{Cov}(\Gamma_j)\, \hat\alpha$, added to the noise of gene $j$;
  • $\alpha$ is estimated with $\mathbb E[\Gamma_j\Gamma_j^\top] = \Gamma_j\Gamma_j^\top + \text{Cov}(\Gamma_j)$ in its normal equations (errors in variables). Along a factor that the loadings barely determine, $\hat\alpha$ then stays small instead of growing without bound.

$\lambda$ cannot replace this term. The term is zero without confounding ($\alpha \approx 0$). And it grows with the latent variance $\sigma_j^2$, not with the Poisson noise, so it weighs most on highly expressed genes: a common factor $\lambda$ gives them too little and the weakly expressed genes too much (§7.3). The Gaussian log-ratio branch (§5.7) has no such covariance and treats its loadings as exact.

4.4 The noise model

The $t$ likelihood. Each $s_j$ rests on a variance estimated from $d$ degrees of freedom (§4.5). Given the prior on the variances, the shrunk estimate follows a $t$ distribution with $d_0 + d$ degrees of freedom (Lemma 1 of Lu & Stephens 2019). With likelihood = :t, the default, the normal densities above are replaced by these $t$ densities.

Inflation adds noise. The inflation $\lambda$ stands for noise that the variance estimate does not see. Scaling the $t$ by $\lambda$ would stretch its tails over this extra noise. Instead, the package adds normal noise of variance $(\lambda - 1) s_j^2$ to the $t$. The sum is approximated by a $t$ with squared scale $\lambda s_j^2$ and the same excess kurtosis, which gives the degrees of freedom

\[\nu_\lambda = 4 + (\nu - 4)\,\lambda^2 \qquad (\lambda > 1;\ \text{otherwise } \nu_\lambda = \nu).\]

Derivation

A $t_\nu$ has excess kurtosis $6 / (\nu - 4)$. Adding independent normal noise does not add kurtosis, but dilutes it: the $t$ part carries a share $1/\lambda$ of the variance, so the excess kurtosis of the sum is $6 / ((\nu - 4)\lambda^2)$. Setting this equal to $6 / (\nu_\lambda - 4)$ gives the formula.

Mixture of $t$. In the Gaussian log-ratio branch (§5.7), vash (§4.5) makes the prior on the variances a mixture. The exact likelihood is then a mixture of $t$, one per variance component $m$, with $2a_m + d$ degrees of freedom. likelihood = :tmix uses this mixture. likelihood = :t uses a single $t$ with averaged degrees of freedom. likelihood = :normal ignores the uncertainty of the variances.

4.5 Variance shrinkage

Each variance $\hat\sigma_j^2$ is estimated from few degrees of freedom: $d = n - k - q - 1$, about six for ten samples. A gene with a small sample variance by chance gets a large, spurious significance. Pooling the variances across genes prevents this.

  • Assumption — $\hat\sigma_j^2 \mid \sigma_j^2 \sim \sigma_j^2\,\chi^2_d / d$.

limma (Smyth 2004) puts one scaled inverse-$\chi^2$ prior on the variances, $\sigma_j^2 \sim d_0 \sigma_0^2 / \chi^2_{d_0}$. It estimates $d_0$ and $\sigma_0^2$ from the moments of $\log \hat\sigma_j^2$. The shrunk variance is

\[\tilde\sigma_j^2 = \frac{d_0 \sigma_0^2 + d\, \hat\sigma_j^2}{d_0 + d} .\]

vash (Lu & Stephens 2016) does for the variances what ash does for the effects. Its prior is a mixture of inverse gammas, with a common scale $c$ and a grid of shapes $a_m$:

\[\sigma_j^2 \sim \sum_m \omega_m\, \text{InvGamma}(a_m,\; a_m c) .\]

limma is the case of one component. A mixture can fit variances that are bimodal or heavy-tailed. The weights $\omega_m$ are estimated by mix-SQP, and $c$ by a one-dimensional search. The shrunk variance is $\tilde\sigma_j^2 = 1 / \mathbb E[\sigma_j^{-2} \mid \hat\sigma_j^2]$.

Where each prior is used.

  • The PLN fit (the default analysis) puts the limma prior inside its own objective (§5.4).
  • The Gaussian log-ratio branch (§5.7) shrinks its variances after the factor analysis, with shrink_var = :vash (default), :limma or :none.

Variances that depend on expression. A prior with one scale pulls every variance towards it. But RNA-seq variances depend on expression, so a common scale shrinks the variances of some genes too much and those of others too little. Both analyses therefore centre the prior on a trend in expression: a local fit over the 20 % of genes nearest in expression (span = 0.2), held flat at the extremes. It is on by default (var_prior = :trend in the PLN fit, var_trend = true in the Gaussian branch). Turned off, every variance is shrunk towards one value, as in limma without trend.
